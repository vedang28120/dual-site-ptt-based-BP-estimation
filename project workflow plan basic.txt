```
             │                       │
```

 The central research question is:

 > **Does combining two optical pulse observations with a controlled physiological perturbation improve subject-independent BP estimation compared with facial rPPG alone?**

 This is important because inter-subject evaluation is substantially harder than random/intra-subject splitting; one published analysis found that performance drops under inter-subject evaluation and warned that some seemingly strong results can partly reflect subject identity rather than BP relationships.  IEEE Xplore

 # 2\. What I would change in your original abstract

 There are two scientific corrections I'd make.

 ### Don't say:

 > “The temporal difference between these signals provides a differential pulse-arrival feature that is less dependent on cardiac timing.”

 Instead:

 > **“The relative timing between spatially separated optical pulse signals provides a cross-site pulse-propagation feature that captures vascular dynamics while reducing reliance on features from a single optical site.”**

 Why?

 Because both signals originate from the same cardiac cycle. The timing difference isn't magically independent of cardiac timing.

 Also, be careful calling it **PTT**.

 Strictly speaking, if you measure a facial pulse and a fingertip pulse, you are measuring a **pulse arrival/delay between two peripheral sites**, not necessarily classical arterial PTT with a known proximal reference. I'd call the feature:

 **Differential Pulse Arrival Time (dPAT)**

 until you establish the physiological interpretation experimentally.

 # 3\. Why the idea is scientifically interesting

 There is already evidence for several individual pieces of your concept.

 ### Smartphone fingertip PPG

 Smartphone camera PPG has already been investigated for BP estimation. One validation study of a smartphone fingertip optical method included 91 subjects and reported mean ± SD errors of approximately 0.5 ± 7.7 mmHg for SBP and 0.4 ± 4.6 mmHg for DBP under its study protocol.  PubMed

 A more recent study involving 127 participants reported smartphone PPG waveform-feature MAEs of 7.75 mmHg SBP and 6.35 mmHg DBP using regression models.  PubMed

 ### Facial rPPG

 Facial video has also been investigated for BP estimation. For example, one study extracted spatial pulse-wave information from facial video and used CNN modelling, reporting MAE around 5.4 mmHg for mean BP in a very small 10-person dataset.  PubMed

 ### Dual-camera pulse timing

 And this is particularly relevant to your idea:

 A **2025 EMBC paper** specifically investigated smartphone dual-camera BP screening using PPG from the **finger and ear**, calculating inter-site PTT and combining it with HR/HRV features. The study involved only 15 participants, so it is early-stage evidence, but it demonstrates that the general direction of smartphone dual-site optical sensing is scientifically plausible.  PubMed

 ### Controlled arm elevation

 Even more interesting for your perturbation idea, research has shown that changing arm position changes peripheral PTT because of hydrostatic pressure effects; one study specifically investigated PTT changes during arm elevation and reported a more pronounced PTT change during elevation than lowering.  PubMed Central (PMC)

 So your idea isn't:

 > “Let's throw random sensors at BP.”

 It can be framed as:

 > **Two spatial optical observations + an experimentally controlled hydrostatic perturbation + multimodal learning.**

 That's a legitimate research hypothesis.

 # 6\. Stage 4 — Differential pulse timing

 Now detect individual pulse beats.

 For each beat:

```
Face pulse                         Finger pulse

       /\                               /\
      /  \                             /  \
_____/    \_____                 _____/    \_____
     ↑                                  ↑
  face foot                         finger foot

           <------ Δt ------>
```

 Calculate:

 $$
dPAT = t_{finger}-t_{face}
$$

 Don't rely on just one beat.

 Use:

```
30-second recording
       ↓
individual beats
       ↓
dPAT for each beat
       ↓
median dPAT
       ↓
dPAT variability
```

 Useful features:

 - median dPAT
- mean dPAT
- standard deviation
- interquartile range
- beat-to-beat variability
- correlation between waveforms
- cross-correlation lag
- normalized cross-correlation
- pulse width
- rise time
- decay time

 # 8\. Estimate the hydrostatic perturbation

 If the vertical height difference is approximately $h$:

 $$
\Delta P \approx \rho g h
$$

 For blood:

 $$
\Delta P \approx 0.74 \times h_{\text{cm}}
$$

 approximately in mmHg.

 So if the hand moves 30 cm upward:

 $$
\Delta P \approx 22 \text{ mmHg}
$$

 This doesn't mean the person's systemic BP suddenly changes by 22 mmHg.

 It means the **local hydrostatic pressure at the elevated limb changes**, producing a measurable vascular response.

 That distinction is extremely important in your presentation.

 The physiological plausibility of using arm position as a perturbation is supported by experimental work on PTT and arm movement.  PubMed Central (PMC)

 # 24\. Another important issue: face-to-finger timing

 Your two signals don't need perfect sample-by-sample synchronization.

 You're interested in pulse timing on the order of tens to hundreds of milliseconds.

 Therefore:

```
timestamp synchronization
+
cross-correlation
+
beat-level alignment
```

 is sufficient for your first prototype.

 You can calculate:

 $$
\tau^* = \arg\max_\tau Corr(P_{face}(t),P_{finger}(t+\tau))
$$

 Then:

 $$
dPAT = \tau^*
$$

 This is actually a nice mathematical element to put on your poster.

 # 30\. Your pitch narrative

 Here's the story I'd tell the judges:

 ### Problem

 > Blood pressure is one of the most frequently measured cardiovascular parameters, but conventional measurement requires a cuff. Smartphone camera methods are attractive, but models based on a single facial rPPG signal can be sensitive to person-specific vascular characteristics, motion, lighting and signal quality.

 Current literature supports the general concern that cuffless BP methods face substantial challenges around calibration, validation and generalization.  PubMed+1

 ### Existing work

 > We previously developed a smartphone facial-rPPG-to-BP system.

 Your existing repository demonstrates that foundation, including mobile acquisition, rPPG extraction, filtering, quality checks and BP inference.  GitHub

 ### Limitation

 > A single facial optical waveform gives us only one view of the cardiovascular pulse.

 ### Our hypothesis

 > What if the smartphone observes the pulse from two locations and deliberately perturbs the vascular system?

 ### Solution

 > We combine facial rPPG, fingertip PPG, cross-site pulse timing and IMU-guided hand elevation with a multimodal AI model.

 ### Research question

 > Can these additional physiological observations improve generalization to people the model has never seen?

 ### Demo

 > Put the phone in front of your face. Place your finger on the rear camera. Raise your hand when prompted. The phone measures multiple physiological signals and produces a BP estimate together with a signal-quality/confidence indicator.

 ### Impact

 > We are not claiming to replace a clinical cuff. We are building and experimentally evaluating a pathway toward accessible smartphone-based BP screening.

 That final sentence is important.

```
Good signals
     ↓
BP estimate
     +
confidence

Bad signals
     ↓
NO RELIABLE ESTIMATE
     ↓

```
